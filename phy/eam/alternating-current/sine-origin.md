---
title: Where the sine comes from
---

::: card
The sine is not a design choice; it falls straight out of how alternating current is generated.
Start with the one fact a generator rests on: a wire moving through a magnetic field has a
voltage pushed along it, but only when it cuts across the field lines.
:::

::: card
The rule behind it is the force a magnetic field puts on a moving charge.[magnetic force](reference:magnetic-force)

$$ F = q\,v\,B \sin\theta $$

The $\sin\theta$ means the field acts only on the part of a motion that crosses it: drag a charge
along the lines and the force is zero; across them, the full push. And the force comes out
perpendicular to both the motion and the field.
:::

::: card
You supply the motion by moving the wire; the magnet supplies the field; together they make the
force that pushes the electrons. For that force to drive current it must point along the wire, so
the wire, its motion, and the field must sit mutually at right angles. Line any two of them up
and it collapses.
:::

::: card
Make the wire a loop and spin it. Each side sweeps around in a circle, so the angle at which it
cuts the field lines changes continuously. Moving straight across the lines, it cuts hardest and
the voltage peaks; a quarter-turn later it moves along them and the voltage is zero. The smooth
slide between those two, every revolution, is the sine.
:::

::: card
A single loop gives a small voltage; wind it into a coil and each turn adds in series, a hundred
turns giving a hundred times the voltage. The energy is not free: the field only converts it, and
the work comes from whatever spins the shaft. Draw current and the generator fights back, harder
to turn.
:::

::: exercise q1
As a generator loop turns, at what point in the revolution is the induced voltage greatest?

::: answer
When the loop's sides are moving straight across the field lines, cutting them hardest. A
quarter-turn later they move along the lines, cut nothing, and the voltage is zero.
:::
:::
