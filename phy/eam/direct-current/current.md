---
title: Current
---

::: card
Get all those free electrons moving the same way and you have a current. Current is not the
electrons themselves; it is the rate at which charge passes a point. Stand at one cross-section
of the wire and count the coulombs going past each second. That is the current, $I$, measured in
**amperes**.
:::

::: card
One ampere is one coulomb per second. Multiply the current by the time and you get the charge
that passed.

$$ I = \frac{Q}{t} \qquad 1\ \text{A} = 1\ \text{C/s} $$
:::

::: card
In every diagram and formula, current is drawn flowing out of the positive terminal, around the
circuit, and into the negative one, as if positive charge were moving. In a metal it is really
the electrons going the other way, from minus to plus.
:::

::: card
The convention was fixed in the 1700s, before electrons were discovered. By the time the mistake
was found, every instrument and textbook already used it. The arithmetic works out the same
either way, so we use the convention and move on.
:::

::: card
If electrons creep a few millimetres a second, why does a lamp light the instant you flip the
switch? Because no electron travels from the switch to the lamp. The wire is already packed end
to end. Push one in at one end and one pops out the other at once, like a marble pushed into a
full tube. The push travels near the speed of light; the electrons barely shuffle.
:::

::: exercise q1
A current of 2 A flows for 5 minutes. How much charge passes a given point?

::: answer
$Q = I t = 2 \times 300 = 600\ \text{C}$. Convert the 5 minutes to 300 seconds first.
:::
:::
