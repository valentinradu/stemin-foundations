---
title: Resistance
---

::: card
Voltage is the push; resistance is what pushes back. As electrons drift through the lattice they
collide with atoms and lose a little energy at each collision. That opposition is **resistance**,
written $R$ and measured in **ohms**, symbol $\Omega$.
:::

::: card
A wide, short pipe passes a lot of flow for a given pressure; a long, narrow one passes far
less. A conductor works the same way: short and thick means low resistance, long and thin means
high. A long thin extension lead runs a heavy load feebly and gets warm, because its resistance
limits the current and the lost energy turns to heat in the wire itself.
:::

::: card
Resistance depends on three things: the material, the length, and the cross-section. Copper has
lower resistance than aluminium for the same dimensions. Longer wire has more; wider wire has
less. The cable sizing rules follow directly: too thin a cable means too much resistance, too
much heat, and not enough current reaching the load.
:::

::: card
A short circuit is the extreme case: a wire bridging two points with no load, resistance near
zero. The current is limited only by the wire itself, which is why it reaches thousands of amps
and the fuse blows. A starter motor drawing 150 A is not a short: its small resistance, 0.08 Ω,
is doing real work, turning electrical energy into torque.
:::

::: exercise q1
Two wires are the same material and length, but one is thicker. Which has the lower resistance?

::: answer
The thicker one. A wider cross-section lets the electrons through more easily, so resistance
falls as the wire gets fatter.
:::
:::
